Universe Creation

The founding brief this entire plan is built from — the non-negotiable creative DNA every season, episode, and AI-generation prompt must inherit.

Premise

One sentence, memorable enough to survive fifty seasons of drift.

A tailor's daughter from Aba rises inside the Big Tech company that once failed her mother — and has to decide, season after season, whether to burn it down or rebuild it into something that finally keeps its promises.

Required registers

The brief specifies these must all be present, baked into the plot rather than bolted onto it: Love, Romance, Infidelity, Suffering, Tragedy, Comedy, Wealth, Power, Intrigue, Secrets, Mystery, Suspense, Flashbacks.

Protagonist mandate

A woman, by design.

Adaeze "Ada" Obi carries the show. Every era's central mystery is discovered through her curiosity, and every romance, rivalry, and betrayal in the ensemble is ultimately measured against whether it helps or hurts the two hundred small businesses she started fighting for in Episode 1.

Scripting & Scene Mandate

Pacing, memory structure, and universal dialogue standards.

  • 3 to 7 scenes per episode — Every ~3-minute episode must be structured into between 3 and 7 distinct scenes with explicit sluglines (INT./EXT. location and time of day), ensuring dynamic visual pacing and rhythmic narrative transitions.
  • At least one flashback event in 4 episodes — Backstory is dramatized directly rather than dumped in exposition; pivotal history (such as the 2019 Aba fire, the original escrow code drafting, or the secret board pact) must appear as dramatized flashback events at least once every four episodes.
  • Dialogue on every shot (100% coverage) — Every single shot must carry spoken dialogue or an internal thought in the heart of the character (indicated via parentheticals such as (internal thought) or (in her heart)). No shot is left silent.

Education mandate

Three teachable moments per episode, dramatized rather than narrated.

  • One business tip — a real, usable piece of small-business practice, demonstrated through action (a character actually reconciling stock, actually opening a storefront, actually escrowing a payment) rather than stated as advice to camera.
  • One relationship insight — about trust, loyalty, honesty, or boundaries — earned by what a character does in the episode, not delivered as a moral at the end.
  • One Good Strategy / Bad Strategy lesson — drawn from Richard Rumelt's book and dramatized through a decision a character actually makes, not quoted as a business-book aphorism. See below.

The Good Strategy / Bad Strategy mandate

Baked in as often as possible — every season card and every Season 1 episode carries one lesson from Richard Rumelt's book, and Era 3 dramatizes the whole thing.

Rumelt's core idea: good strategy has a kernel — a diagnosis of the real obstacle, a guiding policy for dealing with it, and coherent action that actually carries the policy out. Bad strategy is what's left when a leader skips the diagnosis and just lists goals, or hides the missing plan behind impressive-sounding language ("fluff"). Every Ofia character eventually runs into this distinction — a merchant confusing "sell more" for a plan, a rival confusing ambition for strategy, a founder confusing growth for health — and the show never states the lesson outright; a character's choice demonstrates it instead.

The Jobs/Apple parallel — Ofia's turnaround decade

Era 3 (Seasons 21–30) is built as a direct homage to Steve Jobs's 1997 return to Apple, restaged inside Ofia.

  • The sprawl (S21–22). Chasing a Series C, Ofia expands into dozens of half-finished products across every market that asks for one — the platform equivalent of Apple's bloated pre-1997 lineup. No diagnosis, just goals.
  • The ouster (S23). The Adamu Ledger scandal gives the board its excuse: Chiamaka is pushed out for the sprawl she allowed, the same way Apple's board sidelined Jobs in 1985 — for different reasons, the same result.
  • The interim years (S24). A caretaker regime chases every trend and calls it ambition. Growth stalls. Morale collapses. This is bad strategy fully in charge.
  • The return (S25). Chiamaka comes back — not with more ambition, but with less. Her first act is to say "no" to almost everything the company is currently doing.
  • The kernel (S26). She states the diagnosis and the guiding policy out loud, in public, for the first time in the show's history — Rumelt's framework, dramatized directly.
  • Cutting the line (S27). Dozens of products get killed in one season, the way Apple's product line was cut down to a simple 2×2 grid of four machines.
  • One Login (S28). A single unifying campaign — internal and external — replaces years of scattered messaging, Ofia's answer to "Think Different."
  • The comeback (S29–30). The market notices. So does the press. So does everyone who bet against her.

Commercial mandate

Every episode sells a real Ofia product without ever sounding like an ad.

The rule: a product only appears when a character has a real problem it solves in that scene. Comfort Obi's shop survives an over-order because of Ofia ERP, not because a character says the word "ERP" unprompted. See the Content Engine tab for the full checklist.

Key Locations

The recurring physical spaces of the universe.

The Loft — Engineering Floor
Layout: Third floor, exposed concrete, standing desks, a wall-sized screen tracking platform uptime.
Energy: Focused, headphones-on quiet, broken by the occasional groan when a deploy goes sideways.
Story use: Where product decisions get made and unmade in real time.
Detail: A running tally, kept anonymously, of how many times leadership has asked "can we just add a button for that."
Chiamaka's Office
Layout: Top floor, glass-walled corner, visible from the executive floor, sound-insulated.
Energy: Still. The noise of the building disappears the moment the door closes.
Story use: Private confrontations; the conversations that actually change the company's direction.
Detail: One visitor's chair. A second chair only appears for conversations Chiamaka has decided matter more.
The Boardroom
Layout: Top floor, long glass table, a screen for every metric the board wants to see live.
Energy: Formal until it isn't — the room where composure is a costume everyone is wearing.
Story use: Where power gets exercised, challenged, and occasionally lost, on camera for the minutes.
Detail: A seating chart nobody wrote down, that everyone in the room has memorized anyway.
Mama Comfort's Shop, Aba
Layout: A single-room tailor shop, fabric stacked to the ceiling, one sewing machine, one bare bulb.
Energy: Warm, lived-in, a little too quiet since the fire changed how the family talks about the past.
Story use: The show's emotional anchor and its recurring flashback location.
Detail: A hand-painted sign that's been repainted twice, each time a little more hopeful than the last.
A Business Hub Pickup Point, Yaba Market
Layout: A small storefront-sized physical space where any Ofia merchant's customers can collect or drop off goods.
Energy: Practical, busy, the most literal proof the platform works that any character can point to.
Story use: Where digital promises become physical — the scene that sells the product without a single ad line.
Detail: A logbook of pickups that's outgrown three notebooks in six months.

What Ofia is, in one line

The elevator pitch every character believes, in their own words.

Ofia is a multi-tenant business operating ecosystem for African entrepreneurs — discovery (Compass), storefront & checkout (Shop), operations & staff (Office), inventory & accounting (ERP), and a physical layer of pickup and storage (Business Hub) — all under one login, so a small business never has to be "found," "sold," "run," "counted," and "delivered" through five different apps.

Quality Checklist

Run every episode against this before it ships.

  • Does the audience have a reason to watch the next episode?
  • Is there an unanswered question at the end?
  • Does the episode contain between 3 and 7 distinct scenes with proper location/time sluglines?
  • Does every single shot carry spoken dialogue or a character's internal heart-thought (zero silent shots)?
  • Does this episode feature a dramatized flashback event if it is part of the 4-episode flashback cadence?
  • Is exactly one business tip demonstrated through action, not narration?
  • Is exactly one relationship insight earned through a choice a character makes on-screen?
  • Is exactly one Good Strategy / Bad Strategy lesson dramatized through a decision, not stated as an aphorism?
  • Does an Ofia product solve a real problem a character has in this episode — without anyone saying an ad line?
  • Would the audience post about this episode online?

Scaling Rules

How to maintain quality as the series grows from 24 episodes to 1,200+.

  • One new mystery per era. Don't open a new long-running mystery until the previous era's mystery is at its midpoint resolution.
  • Maximum 3 active romance arcs at once. Ada & Tobi, Bubbles & Chidi, and one new arc per era — no more, or none of them land.
  • Return characters before audiences forget them. Anyone absent more than two seasons gets a reintroduction beat.
  • Expand countries in pairs. Each new market introduces one new character who connects to an existing one — the world grows through relationships, not geography.
  • Episode 1 of every season must work as an entry point. A first-time viewer must understand who Ada is and why they should care by minute two.
  • Retire characters with purpose. Any departure (promotion, resignation, death, relocation) must visibly affect at least three other characters.
  • The mystery resolution must earn the build-up. A ten-season mystery needs a revelation that recontextualizes something the audience already saw — never just new information.
  • Never break the ad-free promise of the fiction. A product only appears when it is the actual solution to the scene's actual problem. If it wouldn't hold up as real Ofia documentation, it doesn't go in the episode.
  • Scene structure & universal dialogue coverage. Every episode contains 3–7 scenes, at least one flashback event in every four episodes, and every shot carries spoken dialogue or an internal monologue.
  • The Content Bible is a living document. Update the Character Bible, Relationship Map, and Master Timeline after every episode.

Product-integration rule (the sales mandate)

How the show "sells Ofia to the world" without ever feeling like an ad.

  • Problem first, product second. Establish the character's real pain (can't track stock, can't find customers, can't get paid safely) before the product appears.
  • Show the fix, don't say the feature name in dialogue more than once per episode. Let the interface, the outcome, or a support call (Bubbles) carry the brand.
  • Rotate the product spotlight. Across a season, every module — Compass, Shop, Office, ERP, AI Assistant, Business Hub, Logistics, Follow — gets at least one real moment.
  • Tie the product to the mystery, not just the merchant-of-the-week. The Merchant Protection Fund is both a real Ofia feature and the engine of the entire first mystery.
